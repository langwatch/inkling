# Layer (d): surprise

Good copy says something the reader did not expect, and a model left alone writes the likely line. Layer (d) measures how close a candidate line is to the lines every competitor would write for the same brief. The measurement work, the prototype and its numbers are in [research/surprise.md](../research/surprise.md).

## Design

Per candidate line, for one brief:

1. **Baseline.** Generate 10 lines for the brief and audience with a plain prompt and no house rules, and cache them per brief.
2. **Claim novelty, the main signal.** A judge call asks whether any baseline line makes the same claim as the candidate, with the candidate placed first and then last. The candidate is novel when both answers say "different"; any disagreement counts as related. This measures the stance, which token statistics miss: "You don't need an agent sandbox" uses ordinary words, and the stance is what surprises a sandbox searcher.
3. **Local surprisal, a second signal.** Mean surprisal without the brief, scored against the 10 baseline lines with a small open model on the CPU, plus the difference with the brief. A line below the baseline mean is predictable; a line the brief explains less than any baseline line is off brief.
4. **Reader comprehension, a hard gate.** From layer (e), at least 2 of 3 personas restate the claim. A surprising line readers cannot restate is noise.

## What ships now

Nothing runs yet. The prototype in `research/prototypes/surprisal.py` scores lines with Qwen2.5-0.5B on the CPU, with no API calls.

What the prototype showed on six lines: surprisal given the brief ranked the approved sandbox title as the most predictable line, because it repeats the searcher's words. Raw perplexity would reject the best line, which is why claim novelty leads and surprisal comes second.
