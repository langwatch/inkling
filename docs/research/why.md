# Why agents write bad copy

LangWatch's founder rejected his agent's landing page titles over and over, then wrote a better one himself in minutes: "You don't need an agent sandbox". He asked the agent why it was so bad at titles. He approved six points of its answer and added two more. The ninth comes from a study of model judges.

1. **No test fails on copy.** Coding agents got good by training on tasks with a checkable answer: the tests pass or they don't. Copy has no such check, so how an agent writes is a side effect of the rest of its training. The same holds at work: when a test fails or a screenshot looks wrong, the agent fixes it on its own. A title has no failing test, so the agent stops at "accurate and complete", which is not the same as "a stranger would click".
2. **Likely words are bland words.** A model predicts the likely next words. Good copy surprises the reader, and a surprise is by definition unlikely. Left alone, an agent drifts to the average of all marketing copy.
3. **Training rewarded being fair and safe.** So the agent concedes ("a sandbox runs your agent"), adds balance and hedges. An ad has to take a side, and that cuts against the habit.
4. **The curse of context.** Psychologists call it the curse of knowledge. The agent's context is full of design notes and features, so it writes about what was built. A good writer thinks like two people at once: a reader with zero context, who has only general background, and the expert teaching them. That is hard even for humans. The founder starts from the person typing "agent sandbox" into Google and asks what would stop them.
5. **Punchy became a set of shapes.** The agent reaches for "X does A. Y does B." and "not X, but Y" over and over. The founder learned what lands from people reacting to his posts.
6. **Everything gets in.** A good title cuts to one claim. "You don't need an agent sandbox" works because it says one thing the searcher didn't expect.
7. **Writing from the middle of the thread.** The reader has none of the conversation the agent had, so the first line has to tell a newcomer where they are and why they should care.
8. **Poor theory of mind.** The hard part is holding the whole context and reading as someone who has none of it, at the same time. That is hard for people too, and the model pictures its reader only up to a point. Telling it who reads, or asking it to picture them, is no fix: if a prompt were enough, the labs would have trained it in already. What works is a clear head: a fresh model with zero context reads the text for the first time and says what it notices first. The reader still has to be named, and often there are several: a seller and an engineer for a brochure on a technical product, a lawyer for an order form, an investor for a pitch deck.
9. **Model judges prefer model text.** Ask a model which of two versions is better, and it picks the one a model wrote more often. In [a study from Meta's AI research lab](https://facebookresearch.github.io/RAM/blogs/unslop/) ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38882)), model judges comparing two versions preferred model-written sections of papers over the expert human originals in 63.5% to 84.6% of pairs. A model judge is no test until it agrees with lines people already approved and rejected. See [judge bias](judge-bias.md).

## Why the draft sounds written

A model asked to talk still sounds like a manual, for four more reasons:

1. **Likely relative to what.** The model predicts what is likely in written text that matches the context, and written text is not speech. A context about a lesson, a framework or steps pulls in the register of tutorials, manuals and slide decks: "Next comes", "Finally", "In short". No one talked like that before AI.
2. **Post-training house style.** Raters rewarded answers that looked organised and polished, so the model moved away from its pretraining mix towards signposts, step labels and parallel structure. That style collapsed into one house voice, and AI text on the web now feeds it back into training.
3. **Steps said as orders.** Instructions are the written genre with the most "you" followed by a verb, and assistant answers default to telling the user what to do. So an explanation of a process comes out as a manual: "You start a fresh model. You give it the text." People explaining say "you can start a fresh one", "you could", "we just", "I usually".
4. **Elegant variation.** Training penalised repetition, so the model avoids saying "then" twice and rotates "next", "after that" and "subsequently". People talking just say "then" again.

Inkling turns each point into a step: tests written before the draft for 1 and 4, rules for 2, 3, 5 and 6, a goal with one claim and a named reader for 6, 7 and 8, zero-context reader tests for 4 and 8, and calibration against human verdicts for 9. See the [process](../../README.md#the-process).
